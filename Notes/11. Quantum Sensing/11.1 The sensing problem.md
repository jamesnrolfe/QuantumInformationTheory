We introduce here the primitive task of **quantum sensing**. Very generally, in a sensing task, a system will have some *property* which we would like to learn as *efficiently as possible*. In quantum sensing, we use **quantum probes** in order to learn this property. Remarkably, we see that *entangled* probes provide significant improvements in the precision with which we can learn properties of systems.

At an abstract level, this isn't too dissimilar to stuff we've seen before. It is closely related to the task of [[2.2 Distinguishing orthogonal and non-orthogonal states|distinguishing quantum states]]. As we will see, we can *encode* the property of interest into a state, and hence our ability to learn the property is intimately related to how well we can then distinguish states with different properties. 

It is also related to the [[4.6 Deutsch's problem|Deutsch]] and [[4.7 The Deutsch-Josza problem|Deutsch-Josza]] problems. There, we had a function $f$ from a set of functions which was encoded into a unitary transformation $U_{f}$. Our goal was to determine whether $f$ had a certain property or not i.e. to learn something about $f$. 

The main difference we will see here is that we are typically interested in a *continuous* parameter. Our focus will be on how *sensitive* we can be to *small changes* in this parameter, using quantum probes. This in particular allows us to assess our *resolution*. 

So what actually is the problem?

Let us now introduce it more carefully. We will look at a concrete example to motivate the setup, and then keeping the presentation more abstract thereafter. 

Let's consider that within some region of space, there is a uniform magnetic field, which we would like to determine. We assume that we know the direction of the magnetic field, which is vertically in the $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}$ direction. We also know what the strength of the field *should be*, but we want to be sensitive to *small variations in the field* around this base value. 

The actually physics at play here is unimportant, but say that we have 'spin-$\frac{1}{2}$' particles that have two orthogonal states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. Assuming that we send such particles through the field, and that they spend a time $\tau$ within it, the dynamics can be expressed in terms of the *unitary evolution* that this field will produce, given by

$$
U_{B,\tau}=e^{-iB\tau/\hbar}|0\rangle \langle 0|+e^{iB\tau/\hbar}|1\rangle \langle 1|
$$

which depends on the field strength $B$ and the duration $\tau$. We see in fact that the unitary operator depends only on the product $B\tau$ and so we can simplify, whilst making this more abstract, by substituting in a *phase* $\frac{\phi}{2}=\frac{B\tau}{\hbar}$ so that the evolution is given by

$$
U_{\phi}=e^{-i\phi/2}|0\rangle \langle 0|+e^{i\phi/2}|1\rangle \langle 1|
$$

We assume that we know the duration $\tau$, then *if* we can find $\phi$ we can accurately determine $B$, since they are related via

$$
B=\frac{\hbar \phi}{2\tau}
$$

Furthermore, as we said previously, we assume that we know a value that the field should be, which we denote $B_{0}$. This corresponds to some phase

$$
\phi_{0}=\frac{2B_{0}\tau}{\hbar} 
$$

If we are interested in variations around $B_{0}$, then this corresponds to some scaled variations around $\phi_{0}$. 

So, we have managed to cast the problem directly in terms of this abstract phase $\phi$, rather than in terms of magnetic field and duration. Many other problems can be treated in the same way, so this works as a general method for solving physical problems. We will now focus only on the abstract form (in terms of phase).
