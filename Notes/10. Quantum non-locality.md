In this section, we will see that if Alice and Bob employ a *quantum strategy* to play the [[9.4 The CHSH game|The CHSH game]] then they can win with a higher success rate!

The quantum strategy they will employ is to use an entangled pair of qubits, of which Alice has one, and Bob has the other. When they get given the inputs $x,y$ they choose between two measurement operators to measure to measure on their half of the state. Then, they can use these outcomes to produce the outcomes to the game $a,b$. 

##### The quantum strategy

A more detailed overview of the quantum strategy is shown below.![[Screenshot 2025-03-13 at 12.37.44.png]]
They are both given the state $|\Phi \rangle$ which is the maximally entangled state. When given inputs $x,y$ they measure $A_{x}, B_{x}$ on their half, which produce outcomes $\alpha,\beta=\{ +1,-1 \}$, which they use as shown to produce the outcomes to the game as

$$
a = \begin{cases}
0  & \text{if } \alpha=+1 \\
1 & \text{if } \alpha=-1 
\end{cases}
\quad\quad\quad 
b = \begin{cases}
0  & \text{if } \beta=+1 \\
1 & \text{if } \beta=-1 
\end{cases}
$$

The operators $A_{x}$ and $B_{x}$ are combinations of $X$ and $Z$. The measurements can be conveniently depicted as in the figure below.![[Screenshot 2025-03-13 at 12.41.20.png]]

We have used a [[6opt. The Bloch Sphere|Bloch sphere]] representation here.

To better understand these operators, consider a more general class of operators, which which these are special cases:

$$
S(\theta)=\cos (\theta)Z+\sin \theta(X)
$$

Such operators are Hermitian for all $\theta$, and can be shown to have eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ independent of $\theta$. The corresponding eigenstates are in fact

$$
\begin{align}
| \uparrow _{\theta}\rangle  & =\cos \frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle +\sin \frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle  \\
| \downarrow_{\theta}\rangle  & =-\sin \frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle +\cos \frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle 
\end{align}
$$

As such, the spectral decomposition of $S(\theta)$ is given by

$$
S(\theta)=|\uparrow_{\theta}\rangle \langle \uparrow_{\theta}|-|\downarrow_{\theta}\rangle \langle \downarrow_{\theta}|
$$

where we can verify by expanding out in terms of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ that $S(\theta)=\cos (\theta)Z+\sin \theta(X)$.

We can see that the operators introduced in the figure above are all part of this family of operators, namely

$$
A_{0}=S\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right),\quad A_{1}=S(0),\quad B_{0}=S\left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right),\quad B_{1}=S\left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right)
$$

hence all of Alice and Bob's outcomes $\alpha,\beta \in \{ +1,-1 \}$ as claimed. 

It remains now to calculate the probability of success of the CHSH game using this quantum strategy. Written out in full, the success probability is

$$
\begin{align}
p^{\text{suc}}  &  = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{x,y}P(a\oplus b|x,y) \\
 \\
 & =\frac{1}{4}[P(a\oplus b=0|x,y=0,0)+P(a\oplus b=0|x,y=0,1)+P(a\oplus b=0|x,y=1,0) \\
 &\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad \,+P(a\oplus b=1|x,y=1)]
\end{align}
$$

What we will first show is that the probabilities appearing in this expression, when using the above quantum strategy, are given by

$$
\begin{align}
P(a\oplus b=0|x,y) & =\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle \Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{y}|\Phi \rangle ) \\
P(a\oplus b=1|x,y) & =\frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{y}|\Phi \rangle )
\end{align}
$$

for all $x,y \in \{ 0,1 \}$. These expressions are helpful as they reduce out problem to one of simple calculating the values of $\langle\Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{y}|\Phi \rangle$.

For any state $|\Psi\rangle$ and any pair of operators $A,B$ with eigenvalues $\alpha, \beta=\pm 1$, it holds by definition that

$$
\langle \Psi|A\otimes B|\Psi\rangle =\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha \beta \text{Prob}(\alpha,\beta)
$$
In our case then

$$
\begin{align}
\langle \Psi|A\otimes B|\Psi\rangle  & = \sum_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha \beta P(\alpha,\beta) \\
 \\
 & =P(\alpha,\beta=+1,+1)-P(\alpha,\beta=+1,-1) \\
 &\quad  -P(\alpha,\beta=-1,+1)+P(\alpha,\beta=-1,-1) \\
 \\
 & =P(\alpha = \beta)-P(\alpha \neq \beta) \\
 \\
 & = P(\alpha=\beta)-1+P(\alpha=\beta) \\
 \\
 & =2P(\alpha=\beta)-1
\end{align}
$$

Note that to find what $P(\alpha,\beta)$ is, we need to use the standard quantum mechanical rule: using eigenstates of $A$ and $B$ corresponding to the eigenvalues $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and by looking at the norm of the state after projecting onto these eigenstates. Above however, we did not actually need to calculate these probabilities, we just wrote them abstractly. 

By rearranging the above, we can then find 

$$
P(\alpha=\beta)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \Psi|A\otimes B|\Psi\rangle )
$$

Finally, we connect this back to the strategy we use. We recall that Alice's strategy is to give $a=0$ when the result is $\alpha=+1$, and $a=1$ when $\alpha=-1$ (and similarly for Bob). So, $\alpha=\beta$ implies that $a=b$, which leads to $a\oplus b=0$. 

Putting everything together, in the situation where Alice receives $x$ and Bob receives $y$, Alice measures $A_{x}$ and Bob measure $B_{y}$; whenever their outcomes $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are equal, the bits they produce satisfy $a\oplus b=0$. The above equation gave us the total probability that the outcomes are equal, and so this is equivalent to

$$
P(a\oplus b=0|x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle \Phi|A_{x}\oplus B_{y}|\Phi \rangle )
$$

since this only happens when $a\oplus b=0$ i.e. $\alpha=\beta$.

We can do a very similar approach for the other case $\alpha \neq \beta$ and find that

$$
P(\alpha \neq \beta)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \Phi|A\otimes B|\Phi \rangle) 
$$

and hence

$$
P(a \oplus b=1|x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{y}|\Phi \rangle )
$$

So we have

$$
\begin{align}
P(a\oplus b=0|x,y) & =\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle \Phi|A_{x}\oplus B_{y}|\Phi \rangle ) \\
 \\

P(a \oplus b=1|x,y) & =\frac{1}{2}(1-\langle \Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{y}|\Phi \rangle 
\end{align}
$$

What then remains is to calculate the expectation value $\langle\Phi|A_{x}\otimes B_{x}|\Phi \rangle$ (which appears in both probabilities).

Recalling that all of the operators are from the family $S(\theta)$, finding a general expression for $\langle\Phi|S(\theta)\otimes S(\phi)|\Phi \rangle$ will yield the results we want.

Using the definition of $S(\theta)$, we can say that

$$
\begin{align}
\langle \Phi|S(\theta)\otimes S(\phi)|\Phi \rangle  & = \langle \Phi |[Z\cos \theta+X\sin \theta]\otimes [Z\cos \phi+X\sin \phi]|\Phi \rangle  \\
 \\
 & =\cos \theta \cos \phi \langle \Phi|Z\otimes Z|\Phi \rangle +\cos \theta \sin \phi \langle \Phi|Z\otimes X|\Phi \rangle  \\
 &\quad  +\sin \theta \cos \phi \langle \Phi|X\otimes Z|\Phi \rangle +\sin \theta \sin \phi \langle \Phi|X\otimes X|\Phi \rangle 
\end{align}
$$

So we actually need to evaluate four expectation values. Remember that $|\Phi \rangle$ was the maximally entangled state

$$
|\Phi \rangle = \frac{|0\rangle |0\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}
$$

Hence 

$$
\begin{align}
\langle \Phi|X\otimes X|\Phi \rangle  & = \langle  \Phi| \frac{|1 \rangle |1\rangle +|0\rangle |0\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}=\langle \Phi|\Phi \rangle=1 \\
 \\
\langle \Phi|Z\otimes Z|\Phi \rangle & = \langle \Phi| \frac{|0\rangle |0\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }} = \langle \Phi|\Phi \rangle =1 \\
 \\
\langle \Phi|X\otimes Z|\Phi \rangle  & =\langle \Phi| \frac{|1\rangle |0\rangle -|0\rangle |1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}=\langle \Phi|\Psi\rangle =0 \\
 \\
\langle \Phi|Z\otimes X|\Phi \rangle  & =\langle \Phi| \frac{-|0\rangle |1\rangle +|1\rangle |0\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}=\langle \Phi|\Psi\rangle =0 
\end{align}
$$

where we have used the fact in the last two lines that this new state is orthogonal to $|\Phi \rangle$.

Then, substituting these results back in we find

$$
\langle \Phi|S(\theta)\otimes S(\phi)|\Phi \rangle = \cos \theta \cos \phi-\sin \theta \sin \phi=\cos(\theta-\phi)
$$

which is a lot nicer to use. We can see that if $\theta=\phi$ then Alice and Bob measure with the same operator and $\cos(0)=1$ suggests that $\alpha=\beta$ with certainty. We already observed above that this was the case, and so this makes sense. 

Remember we had the equation 

$$
P(a\oplus b=0|x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle \Phi|A_{x}\oplus B_{y}|\Phi \rangle )
$$

Let's say Alice receives $x=0$ and Bob receives $y=0$, so then Alice measures $A_{0}=S\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right)=X$ and Bob measures $B_{0}=S\left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right)=\frac{X+Y}{\sqrt{ 2 }}$. Hence, the probability of correctly producing bits that satisfy $a\oplus b=xy=0$ is then

$$
\begin{align}
P(a\oplus b=0|x,y=0,0) & =\frac{1}{2}\left( 1+\left\langle  \Phi|S\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\otimes S\left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right) |\Phi  \right\rangle \right) \\
 \\
 & = \frac{1}{2}\left( 1+\cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4} \right) \right) \\
 \\
 & =\frac{1}{2}\left( 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }} \right) \approx 0.854
\end{align}
$$

and so this probability suggests that they win 85% of the time!

If we did this with all the other scenarios, we would get the same probability (*see lecture notes page 72 for direct calculations, but they follow in the exact same way*). 

This then demonstrates conclusively that *quantum mechanics can not be replaced by a deeper local theory*.
