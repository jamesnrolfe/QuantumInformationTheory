#Notes 

The main idea here is to improve the resolution over the case in [[11.2 Single-probe sensing]] by probing the unitary $U_{\phi}$ $n$ times *in parallel*. That is, we will send through $n$ particles instead of just one. 

The simplest $n$ probe state we could consider is to send in $n$ identical qubits in the product state considered before i.e. $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$. We then consider what happens when we apply $U_{\phi}$ on *each copy*.

$$
|\psi_{\phi}^{n}\rangle =(U_{\phi}\otimes\dots \otimes U_{\phi})(|+\rangle |+\rangle \dots|+\rangle )=\left( \frac{e^{\frac{-i\phi}{2}}|0\rangle +e^{\frac{i\phi}{2}}|1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }} \right)^{\otimes n}
$$

>[!help] Note
>Recall that $|x\rangle^{\otimes n}=\prod^{n}_{1}|x\rangle$.

We now measure $X$ on each qubit, and it is natural to add all the outcomes together, meaning we get results ranging from $+n$ (where all measurements are $+1$) to $-n$ (where all measurements are $-1$). The resulting probability distribution is *binomial* i.e. for $-n \leq k \in \mathbb{Z}\leq n$ we have

$$
P(k) = \binom{n}{\frac{n+k}{2}}\left( \cos^{2}\left( \frac{\phi}{2} \right) \right)^{\frac{n+k}{2}}\left( \sin^{2}\left( \frac{\phi}{2} \right) \right)^{\frac{n-k}{2}}
$$

where $\binom{n}{\ell}=\frac{n!}{\ell!(n-\ell)!}$. The *average* and *standard deviation* of this distribution are

$$
\langle X\rangle _{\phi}=n\cos \phi;\quad \Delta X_{\phi}=\sqrt{ n }|\sin \phi|
$$

So, we can think of multiplying the mean and variance by $n$ in the result from [[11.2 Single-probe sensing]]. 

If we consider the specific phase $\phi=\phi_{0}$ again as before, after $N$ repetitions of the procedure (each involving an $n$ qubit state, such that we have a total number of qubits $nN$), then we would obtain an estimate of $x_{0}$ with statistical uncertainty

$$
\Delta x_{0}=\frac{\Delta X_{\phi_{0}}}{\sqrt{ N }}=\sqrt{ \frac{n}{N} }|\sin \phi_{0}|
$$

Combining this with the fact that $\frac{d \langle X\rangle_{\phi}}{d\phi}=-n\sin \phi$, we can see that the resolution becomes

$$
\Delta \phi_{0}=\frac{\Delta x_{0}}{\Big| \frac{d \langle X\rangle _{\phi}}{d\phi}\big|_{\phi=\phi_{0}}\Big|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ nN }}
$$

i.e. we obtain the exact result as before, except now we have $nN$ instead of $N$ (the total number of qubits used in both cases). This is intuitive - by averaging before repeating, we don't gain an advantage over just repeating. This result, that using $n$ unentangled probe states in parallel leads to a precision scaling as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{ n }}$ is referred to as the **standard quantum limit**. 

But, what happens when the probes are entangled? Consider the case when the probes are in the following state:

$$
|\omega^{n}\rangle =\frac{|0\rangle ^{\otimes n}+|1\rangle ^{\otimes n}}{\sqrt{ 2 }}
$$

This state is known as the **GHZ state**. When $n=2$, this is just the *maximally entangled Bell state*. How can we prepare this state? We use the **CNOT** operation! 

In particular, consider preparing $|+\rangle|0\rangle\dots|0\rangle$ and applying CNOTs sequentially between qubits, starting with the first and second, then second and third, then third and fourth etc.. We see that

$$
|+\rangle |0\rangle ^{\otimes n-1} \to \frac{|0\rangle |0\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}|0\rangle ^{\otimes n-2} \to \frac{|0\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}|0\rangle ^{\otimes n-3} \to\dots\to |\omega^{n} \rangle 
$$

i.e. each CNOT grows the initial state into the GHZ state from $|\omega^{k}\rangle$ to $|\omega^{k+1}\rangle$. If we now consider probing $U_{\phi}$ using this GHZ state, we arrive at 

$$
|\omega_{\phi}^{n}\rangle =(U_{\phi}\otimes\dots \otimes U_{\phi})|\omega_{n}\rangle =\frac{e^{-\frac{in\phi}{2}}|0\rangle ^{\otimes n}+e^{\frac{in\phi}{2}}|1\rangle ^{\otimes n}}{\sqrt{ 2 }}
$$

This is somewhat similar to the state above in the non-entangled case, but now we have replaced $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ with $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and $|1\rangle^{\otimes n}$ respectively, and the phase has changed as $\phi \to n\phi$. This already hints of some **amplification**, since we still have orthogonal states, but the phase has becomes $n$ times larger. 

We could consider measuring all $n$ qubits, but we can do something smarter. If we consider applying the preparation procedure for the GHZ states in reverse to the state $|\omega_{\phi}^{n}\rangle$, that is we apply CNOTs to the last and second last, then second last and third last etc., we find that

$$
|\omega_{\phi}^{n}\rangle \to \frac{e^{\frac{-in\phi}{2}}|0\rangle ^{\otimes n-1}+e^{\frac{in\phi}{2}}|1\rangle ^{\otimes n-1}}{\sqrt{ 2 }}|0\rangle  \to \dots\to \frac{e^{\frac{-in\phi}{2}}|0\rangle +e^{\frac{in\phi}{2}}|1\rangle }{\sqrt{ 2 }}|0\rangle ^{\otimes n-1}
$$

That is, we return $n-1$ qubits back to the state $|0\rangle$ and the remaining qubit ends up in the single probe state, with the phase amplified. We can therefore proceed identically to the single probe case, we can measure $X$ on the qubit, and all the analysis from before holds, expect now in all cases we replace $\phi\to n\phi$. This means

$$
\langle X\rangle _{\phi}=\cos n\phi;\quad \Delta X_{\phi}=|\sin n\phi|
$$

Crucially, we now see that our sensitivity to changes in $\phi$ is increased due to the amplification, in particular

$$
\frac{d \langle X\rangle _{\phi}}{d\phi}=-n\sin n\phi
$$

which is crucially now $n$ times larger than it was previously. Putting everything together, our resolution is

$$
\Delta \phi_{0}=\frac{\Delta x_{0}}{\Big| \frac{d \langle X\rangle _{\phi}}{d\phi}\big|_{\phi=\phi_{0}}\Big|}=\frac{\frac{|\sin n\phi_{0}|}{\sqrt{ N }}}{|-n\sin n\phi_{0}|}=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{ N } }
$$

Whereas previously our scaling went by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{ n }}$, we now go much faster to (higher resolutions = low $\Delta \phi_{0}$) zero as $\frac{1}{n}$. So, the entangled strategy is better! The limit now is called the **Heisenberg limit** of quantum sensing - this can be shown to be the *best possible scaling that can be achieved*.
