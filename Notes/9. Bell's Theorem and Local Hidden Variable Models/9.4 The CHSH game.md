#Notes 

In [[9.3 Local hidden variable models]] we ended by suggesting that there was a way to prove quantum mechanics was non-local ([[9.2 Non-locality of quantum theory#^f08d7d|Bell's theorem]]), and to prove it, we would show that certain probabilities $P(a,b|x,y)$ cannot be reproduced by a [[9.3 Local hidden variable models|hidden variables model]]. 

The challenge then becomes - how do we do this? The method to use is fairly simple - if we can identify limitations of the local hidden variables model - which they must obey, and then show that quantum mechanics *do not* obey these limitations, then we have done it! These limitations turn out to be very intuitive, and can be thought of in the context of a cooperative game - the **CHSH game**.

We are going to show that Alice's and Bob's probability of success of winning a carefully constructed constructed co-operative game is limited below a certain threshold, whenever we assume their physics is described by a local hidden variables model. On the other hand, in the next section, we will show that when do this using quantum theory, a larger probability of success can be achieved. 

>[!tldr] The CHSH game (non-mathematical)
>One weekend, Alice and Bob each have a 50% chance of being visited by a friend (Amy or Bill respectively) on Saturday.
>Alice and Bob would like to meet up on Saturday afternoon, but must avoid Amy and Bill meeting (since they are not on speaking terms). 
>Alice and Bob can meet on Thursday to decide in advance what to do (to devise a strategy). They cannot communicate during the weekend (to avoid suspicion from Amy or Bill). 
>Alice and Bob much decide on Saturday afternoon - after Amy and Bill have arrived - between either going to the pub or to the park on Saturday afternoon. 
>They "win" if they meet up when it is safe to do so (one or fewer friends visit) *or* if they avoid each other when both friends visit. 

So, what is the maximum probability $p^\text{suc}$ of achieving a successful outcome i.e. a *win*. 
The difficulty is, that on Saturday morning, when Alice needs to decide where to go, Alice will not know if Bill has come to visit Bob or not (and similarly Bob doesn't know if Amy visited Alice). 

One plan that Alice and Bob could agree upon on Thursday is that Alice will always go to the pub, irrespective of whether Amy visits or not, and Bob goes to the park if Bill visits, and to the pub if he doesn't. 

This plan works in three out of four possible scenarios (the one where it doesn't is if Bill visits but Amy doesn't - then Alice goes to the pub and Bob goes to the park - *lose*). Therefore 

$$
p^\text{suc}=\frac{3}{4}
$$

If Alice and Bob are acting via a local hidden variables model, then there is no better option than this. Hence

$$p^{\text{suc}}_{\text{local}}\leq \frac{3}{4}$$

Now let's consider this more mathematically, to prove this.

>[!tldr] The CHSH game (mathematical)
> Alice and Bob receive bits $x \in \{ 0,1 \}$ and $y\in \{ 0,1 \}$ at random i.e. $P(x,y)=\frac{1}{4}$.
> Alice and Bob must produce bits $a \in \{ 0,1 \}$ and $b \in \{ 0,1 \}$ respectively. 
> Alice and Bob win if the bits they produce satisfy the relation 
> $$a \oplus b = xy$$
> where $\oplus$ is addition modulo 2 (which is essentially an `OR` gate).
> The success probability is given by 
> $$p^{\text{suc}} = \sum_{x,y}P(x,y)P(a \oplus b = xy | x,y)$$
> which can reduced to 
> $$p^{\text{suc}}=P(a \oplus b=xy)$$
> Note that $P(a \oplus b=xy|x,y)$ is shorthand for 
> $$\begin{align}   P(0,0|x,y)+P(1,1|x,y)\quad& \text{if } xy=0 \\
 P(0,1|x,y)+P(1,0|x,y)\quad  & \text{if } xy=1 
\end{align}$$

So what does this all mean? 

An $x$ or $y$ means tells us if the friend visits or not. So for example $x=1$ means that Amy visited Alice, and $y=0$ means Bill did not visit Bob. 

Similarly, an $a$ or $b$ represent where Alice and Bob go, with $0$ being the pub, and $1$ the park. 

But why $a\oplus b=xy$? Well, $a\oplus b$ is 0 if Alice and Bob go to the same place, and 1 if they don't. $xy$ is 1 if and only if *both* friends visit. A win can occur *either* if 1 friend visits and Alice and Bob go to the same place, or if neither friends visit and Alice and Bob go the same place, *or* if both friends visit but they go to different places.

If they go to the same place, $a \oplus b=0$. Then, if both friends visit, $xy=1$, and if 1 or less visit $xy=0$. Clearly, success here is had when $a\oplus b=xy$.

If they go to different places, $a \oplus b=1$. Then, as above, $xy=1$ if both friends visit and 0 otherwise. Clearly again, success here is had when $a \oplus b=xy$.

If Alice and Bob use this local hidden variables mode, then they can never achieve $p^\text{suc}=1$. Let's consider a more general case. 
![[Screenshot 2025-03-13 at 11.11.44.png]]
Here, Alice and Bob receive $x,y\in \{ 0,1 \}$ as before, and pick a predetermined location $\lambda=(a_{0},a_{1})$ resp. $\mu=(b_{0},b_{1})$ (remember, when $x=0$, Alice picks $a_{0}$ etc.). So this is a way of mathematically representing the plan that Alice and Bob come up with on the Thursday night. 

Let us assume for simplicity that only a single $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are used by Alice and Bob, i.e. we assume that the hidden source of variables is *deterministic* - the probability distribution $P_{S}(\lambda,\mu)=1$ for some specific choice of $\lambda=(a_{0},a_{1})$ and $\mu=(b_{0},b_{1})$, so we *definitely* pick the option that we have predetermined, or maybe that Alice and Bob *always* decide on the same fixed plan of action in the above informal form with Amy and Bill. If the source was instead probabilistic, this would correspond to them picking plans at random e.g. by flipping a coin to decide which strategy to use. 

For such a *deterministic* local hidden variables model, to achieve $p^\text{suc}=1$, we need all the win conditions to be met. Remember the conditions for a win.
- Alice and Bob go the same place ($a \oplus b=0$) but one or less friends visit ($xy=0$).
- Alice and Bob go to different places ($a \oplus b=1$) but exactly two friends visit ($xy=1$).
The following four conditions must necessarily hold.

$$
\begin{align}
a_{0}\oplus b_{0}=0 &  \\
a_{0}\oplus b_{1}=0 &  \\
a_{1}\oplus b_{0}=0  &  \\
a_{1}\oplus b_{1}=1 & 
\end{align}
$$

>[!help] Note
>Remember our notation. Above we are essentially writing out in full
>$$a_{x}\oplus b_{y}=xy$$
>for all the $x,y$ combinations.

Now we want to show that there is no possible assignment of values for $a_{0},a_{1},b_{0},b_{1}$ that simultaneously satisfy all of the above conditions. Let's proceed by adding together the equations. In this context, we should add them in modulo two.

$$
\begin{align}
(a_{0}\oplus b_{0})\oplus(a_{0}\oplus b_{1})\oplus(a_{1}\oplus b_{0})\oplus(a_{1}\oplus b_{1}) & = 0 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 \\ \\


 \Rightarrow\quad (a_{0}\oplus b_{0})\oplus(a_{0}\oplus b_{1})\oplus(a_{1}\oplus b_{0})\oplus(a_{1}\oplus b_{1}) & =1 \\
 \\
0 & =1
\end{align}
$$

which is clearly not possible. Therefore, all four equations cannot be satisfied. So, *any deterministic* model must lose the game for at least one pair of values of $x,y$. So, the probability of success is bounded by 

$$p^{\text{suc}}(\lambda,\mu)=P(a\oplus b=xy|\lambda,\mu) \leq \frac{3}{4}$$

It turns out that if we allow a probabilistic source, akin to Alice and Bob randomly choosing a strategy on Thursday, this doesn't help.

$$\begin{align}
p^{\text{suc}}_{\text{local}}  & = \sum_{\lambda,\mu} P_{S}(\lambda,\mu)p^{\text{suc}}(\lambda,\mu) \\ \\
 & \leq \sum_{\lambda,\mu} P_{S}(\lambda,\mu) \cdot \frac{3}{4} =\frac{3}{4}
\end{align}$$

where have essentially compute the expectation value of $p^\text{suc}$. 

In the next section, we can see that by using quantum mechanics, we can up this success probability to approximately 85% - hence proving that quantum mechanics cannot be described in this way.
