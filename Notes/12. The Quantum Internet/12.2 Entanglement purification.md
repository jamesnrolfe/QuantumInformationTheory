#Notes 

>[!summary] 
> Here, we can improve the quality of our entangled qubits. By applying an 'error correcting' procedure on pairs of qubits, we can figure out when an error occurred in entanglement. 

In [[12.1 Entanglement swapping and quantum repeaters]] we saw that we could overcome the challenge of distributing entanglement over long distances using quantum repeaters. In that section, however, we made the rather unrealistic assumption that the quantum states were *perfect*. This is a major problem for quantum communication, since we cannot make *noise-free* qubits. However, we can do something - a process known as **entanglement purification**. 

To explain the basic idea, let's consider the process of creating and distributing an entangled quantum state. In this process, certain errors can occur. In particular, let's assume there is some chance that a qubit flips its state from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$ at random.

More precisely, let's imagine that with probability $p$, the operator $X$ is applied to each qubit in the state $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$. We can outline what happens with what probability in a table, shown below.

| Probability | Outcome                           | State after                  |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| $p^{2}$     | Both qubits flip                  | $(X\otimes X)\ket{\Phi_{I}}$ |
| $p(1-p)$    | First qubit flips, second doesn't | $(X\otimes I)\ket{\Phi_{I}}$ |
| $(1-p)p$    | Second qubit flips, first doesn't | $(I\otimes X)\ket{\Phi_{I}}$ |
| $(1-p)^2$   | Nothing happens                   | $\ket{\Phi_{I}}$             |

Due to this random process, we end up with the following **ensemble**
$$
\{ |\Phi_{I}\rangle , (1-p)^{2}+p^{2}; |\Phi_{X}\rangle ,2p(1-p) \}
$$

>[!help] Note
>This is basically saying that two outcomes lead to the same result $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$, and we add those probabilities ($p^{2}$ and $(1-p)^{2}$) to get the total probability of being in this state, and the other two probabilities lead to the state $|\Phi_{X}\rangle$ and we sum their probabilities (both $p(1-p)$) to get the total probability of this outcome.
>We write this as an ensemble:
>$$\{ \text{outcome 1}, \text{probability of outcome 1};\quad   \text{outcome 2}, \text{probability of outcome 2} \}$$

Hence, we can write this as a [[5.1 The density operator|density operator]]:
$$
\rho=q|\Phi_{I}\rangle \langle \Phi_{I}|+(1-q)|\Phi_{X}\rangle \langle \Phi_{X}|
$$
This density operator is a *more accurate* description of the state Alice and Bob would state after distributing entanglement between themselves. Note that if $q=1$, then we recover the state initially shared (with no errors). We are primarily interested in the cases where $q \approx 1$ such that there is only a small chance of the qubits flipping. 

Let's now assume that Alice and Bob have created and distributed two copies of this state, and so share $\rho \otimes \rho$, with each of them holding two qubits. We will let them interact locally with their two qubits (e.g. applying unitaries, performing measurements), and also to *communicate classical information*. Both of these can be thought of as 'cheap' things to do, and they don't require entanglement between Alice and Bob (remember, this is what we are treating as the 'special resource').

After they have finished, we would like them to have produced a single pair of qubits in an entangled state, which crucially is *more highly entangled* than $\rho$. We will allow them to do this *probabilistically*; that is, sometimes it will be higher, and other times it can be lower. In the case that it is lower, they can simply discard their qubits and start again, eventually achieving higher entanglement. Essentially, it only has to be possible for it to be achievable. 

The procedure they follow is quite simple.
1. Alice and Bob both individually apply the CNOT unitary to their pair of qubits.
2. They each measure $Z$ on their second qubit, obtaining the result $+1$ or $-1$. 
3. They communicate classically, telling each other the result of their measurement. If they obtain the *same* result, then they keep their first qubit. If they obtain *different* results, then they have failed, and the procedure must be restarted.

Let's see how this works. Consider what happens if they share the state $|\Phi_{I}\rangle|\Phi_{I}\rangle$, which they share with probability $q^{2}$. This first $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$ is their actual state, and the second is the copy. We can expand this out as
$$
|\Phi_{I}\rangle |\Phi_{I}\rangle =\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle +|0\rangle |0\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle )
$$
 Then, we do step 1 i.e. apply a CNOT to both sets of qubits:
$$
(\text{CNOT}\otimes \text{CNOT})|\Phi_{I}\rangle |\Phi_{I}\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle +|0\rangle |0\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle |1\rangle +|1\rangle |1\rangle |0\rangle |0\rangle ) =|\Phi_{I}\rangle |\Phi_{I}\rangle 
$$
>[!help] Note
>The CNOT's here act between the first and third, and second and fourth qubits, since these are the qubits held by Alice and Bob respectively.

We can see that we essentially do nothing - they will always obtain the same outcome (i.e. 50% of the time measure $+1$ and 50% of the time measure $-1$). When they communicate they will never fail, and so they can *always* keep their first pair of qubits, which are in the state $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$. 

>[!help] Note
>To see how they get their measurements, remember that they measure $Z$ on the second qubit only (here $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$). 

We can now move onto the case where they share $|\Phi_{I}\rangle|\Phi_{X}\rangle$, which they share with probability $q(1-q)$. In this case, applying the CNOTs also doesn't change the state. However, since the second pair of qubits are in a different state $|\Phi_{X}\rangle$ Alice and Bob will obtain different measurement results. Thus, when they communicate in step 3, they will fail *all* of the time, and always discard their remaining qubits. 

The next case is $|\Phi_{X}\rangle|\Phi_{I}\rangle$, which they share with probability $q(1-q)$. After applying the CNOTs, we end up in the state $|\Phi_{X}\rangle|\Phi_{X}\rangle$. When Alice and Bob measure, they will always obtain different results, and hence they will always fail. This is good, since the first pair was in $|\Phi_{X}\rangle$, which contains flips and so we didn't want that anyway. 

The last state to check is $|\Phi_{X}\rangle|\Phi_{X}\rangle$, which they share with probability $(1-q)^{2}$. After applying the CNOTs, they end up in the state $|\Phi_{X}\rangle|\Phi_{I}\rangle$. The second pair is in the state $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$ and so Alice and Bob always obtain the same outcomes, and hence they never fail. 

Putting this all together, in the second and third cases, we always fail, and in the first and fourth, we always succeed. The total probability to succeed is then $q^{2}+(1-q)^{2}$, and so the state they share, in the cases of successes, is the *renormalised* state
$$
\rho'=\frac{q^{2}|\Phi_{I}\rangle \langle \Phi_{I}|+(1-q)^{2}|\Phi_{X}\rangle \langle \Phi_{X}|}{q^{2}+(1-q)^{2}}
$$
which is indeed a normalised density operator. We see that the net effect of the protocol was to remove certain contributions to the density operator, and it can be viewed as a type of probabilistic *filtering process*. Notably, the state above is of the form 
$$
\rho=q'|\Phi_{I}\rangle \langle \Phi_{I}|+(1-q')|\Phi_{X}\rangle \langle \Phi_{X}|
$$
as given before, just scaled via
$$
q'= \frac{q^{2}}{q^{2}+(1-q)^{2}}
$$
We will have improved entanglement if $q' > q$. We can do this via direct calculation, and it comes out that
$$
q'>q\quad \text{if}\quad \frac{1}{2}<q<1
$$
In other words, whenever the state $|\Phi_{I}\rangle$ was more likely that $|\Phi_{X}\rangle$, we can improve the entanglement. 

There is also nothing to stop us applying the procedure multiple times. That is, we could imagine distributing our four pairs of qubits, and applying the above procedure twice, on a pair at a time. If both procedures succeed, we could then apply the procedure a third time, now on the pair of remaining qubits from the two original pairs. If this procedure also succeeds, then we have purified the entanglement twice. Significant gains can be had this way (albeit that more gains have a lower probability of occurring).
